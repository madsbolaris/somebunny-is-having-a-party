## General Questions

### Q: How long does a complete evaluation take?

**A**: Expect **50-75 minutes** for a thorough evaluation:
- Phase 1 (Initial Analysis): 10-15 minutes
- Phase 2 (Card-Level Evaluation): 30-45 minutes (evaluating all 99 cards)
- Phase 3 (Overall Assessment): 10-15 minutes

For experienced evaluators familiar with the rubric, this can drop to 30-40 minutes.

---

### Q: Can I evaluate my own deck?

**A**: Yes, but be aware of **confirmation bias**. You may:
- Overestimate synergies because you know the combo lines
- Underestimate weaknesses because you know how to play around them
- Rate your deck higher than an objective evaluator would

**Recommendation**: Evaluate your own deck, then have someone else evaluate it independently. Compare results and discuss discrepancies.

---

### Q: What if my quantitative score and tier characteristics don't align?

**A**: **Favor the tier characteristics over the raw number**. The quantitative score is a guide, not an absolute truth.

**Example**: A deck scores 7.2 quantitatively but has characteristics of Power 6 (wins turn 11, minimal tutors, casual interaction). Rate it Power 6-7 and note the discrepancy in your summary.

**Why this happens**:
- Some metrics may not capture the deck's actual performance
- Certain archetypes (stax, group hug) don't fit standard scaling
- The deck may have imbalanced strengths/weaknesses

---

### Q: Should I use half-points (e.g., 6.5, 7.5)?

**A**: **Yes, for edge cases** that fall clearly between two tiers.

**When to use half-points**:
- Quantitative score is .3-.7 (e.g., 7.5)
- Deck has characteristics of both adjacent tiers
- You want to signal "upper 7" vs. "lower 7" for matchmaking

**When NOT to use half-points**:
- Don't use for every deck (most should be whole numbers)
- Don't use quarter-points (7.25, 7.75) — too granular

---

### Q: What if the deck owner disagrees with my rating?

**A**: This is common. **Follow this process**:

1. **Review your evaluation together**: Walk through each phase and explain your reasoning
2. **Identify specific disagreements**: Is it speed? Interaction? Synergy?
3. **Compare to reference decks**: "Your deck is slower than the Power 7 Kinnan example, which suggests Power 6-7"
4. **Acknowledge subjectivity**: Some assessments (especially qualitative) are judgment calls
5. **Offer to re-evaluate**: If they provide compelling evidence, adjust your rating

**Common owner biases**:
- **Optimism bias**: Owners remember their best games, not average performance
- **Sunk cost**: "I spent $2000 on this, it must be Power 9!"
- **Complexity confusion**: "This deck is hard to pilot, so it must be powerful"

**Be diplomatic**: The goal is accurate matchmaking, not defending your rating.

---

### Q: How often should I re-evaluate a deck?

**A**: Re-evaluate when:
- **Major upgrades** (e.g., adding Mana Crypt, optimizing mana base)
- **Strategy change** (e.g., shifting from combat to combo)
- **New set releases** that significantly impact the deck
- **Meta shifts** that change the deck's relative power
- **Every 6-12 months** for active decks, even without changes (format power creep)

Minor tweaks (swapping 1-2 cards) usually don't warrant full re-evaluation.

## Special Archetypes

### Q: How do I rate Stax decks?

**A**: Stax decks often rate **1-2 tiers below their actual threat level** using standard metrics because they have low threat density and slow win conditions.

**Adjustments**:
- **Don't penalize low threat density**: Stax wins by locking opponents, not deploying threats
- **Emphasize interaction and resilience**: Count stax pieces as "interaction"
- **Assess lock quality**: Can it lock down a table of similar-power decks?
- **Consider social factor**: Stax draws disproportionate hate (note in summary)

**Example**:
- **Derevi Stax** quantitatively scores 6.5 but effectively plays at Power 7-8 tables
- Rate it **Power 7** and note: "Stax archetype—draws more hate than metrics suggest"

---

### Q: How do I rate Group Hug decks?

**A**: Group Hug decks **don't fit traditional power scaling** because they accelerate everyone.

**Approach**:
- **If symmetrical acceleration** (Howling Mine, Rites of Flourishing): Rate based on the deck's own win condition, note that it helps opponents equally
- **If asymmetrical** (Minds Aglow + Psychosis Crawler, Heartbeat of Spring + Omnath): Rate higher because the deck breaks parity
- **If kingmaking** (deck explicitly chooses winners): Note this in summary; some playgroups ban this

**Example**:
- **Symmetrical Group Hug** (Phelddagrif): Power 4-5, but note "accelerates all players equally—may enable higher-power decks to win faster"
- **Asymmetrical Group Hug** (Kynaios and Tiro with win cons): Power 6-7, normal evaluation applies

---

### Q: How do I rate Chaos decks?

**A**: Chaos decks (Warp World, Scrambleverse, coin flips) are similarly **difficult to rate**.

**Approach**:
- **Assess impact on game determinism**: Does it make games random or just chaotic?
- **Rate based on whether it wins**: Pure chaos with no win condition = Power 2-4
- **Note social contract**: Many playgroups dislike chaos; note this in summary

**Example**:
- **Norin the Wary chaos**: Power 3-4, note "pure chaos, no clear win condition, may frustrate opponents"
- **Yidris cascade chaos with win cons**: Power 6-7, normal evaluation applies

---

### Q: How do I rate Voltron decks in multiplayer?

**A**: Voltron struggles in multiplayer (needs to kill 3 opponents at 40 life each = 120 total damage, or 63 commander damage total).

**Adjustments**:
- **Assess one-shot potential**: Can it kill a player in one attack (21+ commander damage)?
- **If yes**: Rate normally based on speed to lethal
- **If no**: Rate 1-2 tiers lower than goldfish suggests (needs multiple turns to kill each opponent)
- **Extra combat steps**: Count these highly (Aggravated Assault, Sword of Feast and Famine)

**Example**:
- **Sram Voltron** (can one-shot with double strike): Goldfish turn 6, rate Power 6-7
- **Uril Voltron** (needs 2-3 swings per player): Goldfish turn 8, rate Power 5-6 (struggles in multiplayer)

---

### Q: How do I rate Aristocrats decks?

**A**: Aristocrats (sacrifice synergies, Blood Artist effects) rate normally—treat them as combo-control.

**Evaluation tips**:
- **Identify win conditions**: Usually combat + drain effects (Blood Artist, Zulaport Cutthroat)
- **Assess combo potential**: Infinite sac loops (Gravecrawler + Phyrexian Altar + Blood Artist)
- **Rate interaction**: Usually moderate (board wipes synergize with death triggers)

**Example**:
- **Meren Aristocrats**: Power 6-7, focused strategy with value grind + drain win cons

## Budget Considerations

### Q: Should I rate budget decks lower?

**A**: **No**. Rate the strategy and execution, not the price tag.

**Correct approach**:
- A well-built $200 deck at Power 6 is still Power 6
- Use **budget notation** ($ to $$$$) to communicate financial investment separately
- Note in summary: "Power 6 ($$)—room for significant upgrades with investment"

**Why this matters**:
- Budget players deserve accurate matchmaking too
- A budget Power 6 should play against other Power 6 decks, not get pubstomped by Power 8
- Budget constraints ≠ poor deck building

---

### Q: What if a deck is missing obvious staples due to budget?

**A**: **Note this in your summary** but rate the deck as-is.

**Example**:
- Kinnan without Mana Crypt/Vault: Rate Power 7 (based on current performance)
- Note: "Power 7 ($$$)—would be Power 8-9 with Mana Crypt, Gaea's Cradle, and optimized mana base"

**This helps**:
- Deck owner understands upgrade path
- Opponents know what to expect currently
- Clear communication about potential power ceiling

---

### Q: What if a deck has expensive cards but is poorly built?

**A**: **Rate based on actual performance**, not card prices.

**Example**:
- Deck has Mana Crypt, ABUR duals, but terrible synergy and no clear win condition
- Rate Power 4-5 despite expensive cards
- Note: "Power 5 ($$$$)—expensive cards present but strategy is unfocused"

**Common scenario**: Someone buys expensive singles thinking it will automatically make their deck powerful, but doesn't optimize the strategy.

## Meta and Matchmaking

### Q: Should I rate decks differently for different metas?

**A**: **No—use a single universal rating**. But note meta-dependent performance in your summary.

**Approach**:
- Rate the deck objectively using the rubric
- Note in summary: "Power 7—well-positioned against combo-heavy metas (high interaction), may struggle against stax"

**Why universal ratings**:
- Consistency across evaluators and playgroups
- Metas change; deck ratings shouldn't fluctuate constantly
- Meta notes provide context without changing the core rating

---

### Q: What power level range is best for matchmaking?

**A**: **±1 power level** produces the most balanced games.

**Examples**:
- Power 6 deck → Pair with Power 5-7 decks
- Power 8 deck → Pair with Power 7-9 decks
- Power 10 deck → Pair with Power 9-10 only (cEDH)

**Why ±1**:
- ±2 or more creates imbalances (Power 5 vs. Power 8 is a pubstomp)
- Exactly matched (Power 6 vs. Power 6 only) is too restrictive
- ±1 allows variety while maintaining competitiveness

---

### Q: Can a Power 7 deck beat a Power 9 deck?

**A**: **Yes, occasionally**, but it's statistically unlikely.

**Factors**:
- **Variance**: Power 9 mulligans to 4, Power 7 gets perfect hand
- **Politics**: Multiplayer table focuses the Power 9 deck
- **Matchup-specific**: Power 7 stax deck hoses Power 9 combo deck

**But on average**, Power 9 should win significantly more often. If a "Power 7" consistently beats Power 9 decks, it's probably misrated.

## Scoring Edge Cases

### Q: What if a deck has a 2-card infinite combo but no tutors?

**A**: **Rate threat density based on redundancy and consistency**, not just combo compactness.

**Example**:
- 2-card infinite combo (Power 8-9 in isolation)
- 0 tutors, no redundancy (Power 4-5 consistency)
- **Overall threat density**: Power 5-6 (compact combo, but inconsistent)

**Rationale**: A 2-card combo you never draw is no better than a 4-card combo.

---

### Q: What if a deck can win turn 3 but only 10% of the time?

**A**: **Use average win turn, not best-case scenario**.

**Example**:
- Best-case: Turn 3 (10% of games)
- Average: Turn 8 (typical games)
- **Speed score**: 7 (based on turn 8, not turn 3)

**Rationale**: Power level reflects typical performance, not nut-draw potential.

---

### Q: What if a deck has high interaction but it's all narrow (e.g., only creature removal)?

**A**: **Penalize in the "coverage" assessment** of interaction quality.

**Example**:
- 12 interaction pieces (high count)
- All creature removal, no artifact/enchantment hate, no counterspells
- **Interaction score**: 6-7 (high count but poor coverage)

**Rationale**: Narrow interaction can't answer all threats, reducing effectiveness.

---

### Q: How do I handle partner commanders in the evaluation?

**A**: **Evaluate the deck holistically**, not each commander separately.

**Approach**:
- Assess how both partners contribute to the strategy
- Determine if the deck relies on one, both, or neither
- Rate commander reliance accordingly (Critical / Important / Supportive / Color Identity Only)

**Example**:
- **Thrasios + Tymna**: Both contribute (Thrasios = outlet, Tymna = card draw), but deck can function without them → Important
- **Kraum + Ludevic**: Mostly for colors, minimal synergy → Color Identity Only

## Common Disagreements

### Q: Someone rated a deck Power 8, but I think it's Power 6. How do we resolve this?

**A**: **Walk through the evaluation together** and identify where you disagree.

**Process**:
1. Compare quantitative scores category by category
   - "I scored speed as 6 (turn 9), you scored 8 (turn 5)—let's goldfish test to verify"
2. Compare to reference decks
   - "This deck is slower than the Power 7 Kinnan example, which wins turn 7"
3. Discuss qualitative adjustments
   - "You added +1 for meta positioning, but why?"
4. Find common ground
   - "We agree it's Power 6-8 range; let's settle on Power 7"

**If still disagree**: Get a third-party evaluation from another experienced player.

---

### Q: Why do cEDH players rate their decks 9-10, but my "optimized" deck is rated 7?

**A**: **cEDH Power 9-10 is a HUGE jump** from Power 7-8. The gap between Power 7 and Power 10 is larger than Power 1 to Power 7.

**Key differences**:
- **Speed**: Power 7 wins turn 7; Power 10 wins turn 2-3
- **Fast mana**: Power 7 has 4-6 pieces; Power 10 has 8+
- **Cost**: Power 7 is $600-2000; Power 10 is $5000-10000+
- **Interaction**: Power 7 has 10 pieces; Power 10 has 15+ including free spells

"Optimized" doesn't automatically mean cEDH. Most "optimized" casual decks are Power 6-8.

---

### Q: My playgroup thinks all combo decks are cEDH. Is that true?

**A**: **No**. Combo exists at all power levels.

**Examples**:
- **Power 4**: 4-card combo, no tutors, wins turn 14 (casual combo)
- **Power 6**: 3-card combo, 2 tutors, wins turn 10 (focused combo)
- **Power 8**: 2-card combo, 4 tutors, wins turn 6 (optimized combo)
- **Power 10**: 2-card combo, 8 tutors, wins turn 3 (cEDH combo)

**Misconception**: "Infinite combo = cEDH." Reality: It's about speed, consistency, and interaction density.

## Rubric Maintenance

### Q: What if a new set adds power creep?

**A**: **Re-calibrate reference decks** if power creep is significant.

**Process**:
1. Identify if new cards fundamentally shift power levels (e.g., "new Sol Ring at 0 CMC")
2. If yes, update tier definitions to account for new baseline
3. Re-evaluate reference decks with new standards
4. Communicate changes to your playgroup/community

**Example**: If Wizards prints a new 0-CMC mana rock that every deck runs, fast mana counts shift upward and tier definitions adjust.

**Note**: Power creep is gradual. Major recalibration should happen **annually at most**, not with every set.

---

### Q: Should I adjust the rubric for my local playgroup preferences?

**A**: **Yes, but document the changes** so everyone understands the modified standards.

**Common adjustments**:
- **Social contract rules**: "Our playgroup bans infinite combos, so we rate combo potential differently"
- **House rules**: "We allow Primeval Titan, so green ramp decks are stronger in our meta"
- **Budget tiers**: "Most of our group plays $200 budget decks, so we focus on Power 4-6 range"

**Keep the core structure** (three phases, quantitative scoring) but adjust weights or tier definitions as needed.

---

### Q: How do I handle new archetypes that don't fit the rubric?

**A**: **Document the edge case** and share it with the rubric maintainers.

**Process**:
1. Evaluate the deck as best you can using the rubric
2. Note where the rubric doesn't fit (e.g., "Energy mechanic doesn't map to synergy tiers cleanly")
3. Make a judgment call and document your reasoning
4. Propose an update to the rubric if the archetype is common enough

**Example**: When Partners were introduced, the rubric didn't account for two commanders. Evaluators added guidance for assessing partner reliance.
